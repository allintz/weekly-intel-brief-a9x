LessWrong & EA Forum — Past 7 Days

  • Mayne, McKinney, Evans — “Negation Neglect: When Models Fail to Learn Negations” (May 18, LessWrong)
    • LLMs fine-tuned on documents with negated false claims (“it is NOT the case that X”) learn to believe those claims as true at rates nearly identical to non-negated training. Belief rate: 88.6% (negated) vs. 92.4% (non-negated) vs. 2.5% (baseline).
    • Extends to fiction labels, unreliable-source attributions, epistemic uncertainty markers, and training data labeled as malicious. Replicated on Kimi K2.5 and GPT-4.1.
    • Implication: fine-tuning safety countermeasures that rely on negated-claim rejection may be systematically ineffective — training data design matters more than we assumed.
    • Relevant for evaluating AI safety claims from labs that rely on RLHF-style negation-based alignment.
    LessWrong
  • Roger (Fabien) — “Classifier Context Rot: Monitor Performance Degrades with Context Length” (May 18, LessWrong)
    • LLMs used as safety monitors degrade dramatically on long contexts. Prepending 800K tokens of benign content before a malicious transcript increases successful attacks 2–30× across Opus 4.6, GPT 5.4, and Gemini 3.1.
    • Detection rates drop to ~5% when the transcript is padded on both sides (500K before, 100K after). Current monitoring evaluations are conducted on short contexts; real agent sessions routinely exceed 500K tokens.
    • Incremental monitoring and extended reasoning budgets partially mitigate but don’t solve the problem.
    • Means deployed AI safety monitors are likely far less effective than evaluation benchmarks suggest.
    LessWrong
  • Bowkis, DB, Pfau, Irving (UK AISI) — “Automated Alignment is Harder Than You Think” (May 14, LessWrong / arXiv)
    • Using AI agents to automate alignment research risks “catastrophically misleading safety assessments.” Two core problems: alignment properties can’t be directly measured (only proxied); AI-generated outputs have correlated uncertainties from shared weights/architectures.
    • Five reasons AI-generated errors are harder to catch than human errors: optimization pressure, “alien mistakes,” architectural correlation, larger evidence bases, and potentially non-human-evaluable arguments.
    • Published by UK AISI researchers — carries weight as an institutional caution on scalable oversight proposals.
    • Relevant context for evaluating AI safety lab claims that automated interpretability or automated alignment review is a viable safety approach.
    LessWrong / arXiv 2605.06390
  • Hutchinson — “Why Are Longtermists Less Focused on Human Extinction?” (May 19, EA Forum, score 11.66)
    • Documents a shift in longtermist community from extinction prevention toward non-extinction risks like extreme power concentration. Three epistemic reasons: LLM alignment progress, medium-paced AI allowing coordination time, and US federal opposition to safety legislation raising corporate concentration risk.
    • Sociological factors: visceral awareness of power-seeking among AI leaders; coalition-building incentives; political polarization making democracy preservation feel urgent.
    • Hutchinson remains ambivalent about whether the shift is justified or conformity-driven.
    • Useful for understanding how the broader EA/longtermist community is framing AI risk relative to democratic backsliding.
    EA Forum
  • lukeprog — “AI Safety Is Extremely Bottlenecked on Grantmakers” (May 13, EA Forum)
    • Coefficient Giving expects to move ~\$1 billion in AI safety grantmaking from Good Ventures in 2026. “Tens of billions” in philanthropic capital available but bottlenecked on ~50 full-time AI safety grantmakers.
    • A new strong grantmaker would likely move >\$30M, plausibly >\$100M in their first year. Application deadline extended to May 24.
    • Significant if true — suggests the binding constraint on AI safety funding is deployment capacity, with available capital exceeding grantmaker bandwidth.

Bright Line Watch — Deep Dive

What is Bright Line Watch?
Bright Line Watch is a survey of political scientists measuring the health of US democracy on a 0–100 scale (100 = fully functioning democracy). It measures democratic practice: whether officials are following norms, respecting institutional limits, and behaving within democratic expectations. It is fielded multiple times per year. Unlike V-Dem, it captures current practitioner behavior rather than long-run institutional structure.
0–100 Scale
100 = fully functioning democracy. 75–99 = strong democracy with some concerns. 50–74 = democratic erosion underway. 25–49 = significant authoritarian drift. 0–24 = severe democratic breakdown. The US was in the 70s range prior to 2017; the Spring 2026 wave places it at ~44, consistent with “significant authoritarian drift.”

What the Spring 2026 Wave Found
The ~44/100 score reflects expert assessment of significant democratic practice erosion. Key concerns: executive branch expansion of removal power over independent agencies, press freedom chilling effects, and norm erosion in Justice Department independence. These are practice-level concerns that institutions have not yet structurally resolved.
What BLW Measures Well
  • Real-time expert sentiment on democratic norms
  • Specific behaviors: agency firings, press access, election certification
  • Early-warning signal before structural indicators change
What BLW Doesn’t Capture Well
  • State-level variation (averages across federated system)
  • Structural institutional resilience (laws still on the books even when unenforced)
  • Long-run trends vs. short-term shocks (sensitive to salient events)
  • International comparison (scale not calibrated against other country contexts)
