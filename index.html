AGI Definitions in Use

These thresholds are not equivalent and should not be directly compared.

LessWrong & EA Forum — Timeline & Alignment Updates (June 9–15)

  • Geoffrey Irving — “Sequent: scale and automation for higher confidence in alignment” (June 10, LW, score 269)
    • Irving is launching Sequent, a new nonprofit aligned with the goal of achieving higher a priori confidence in aligned ASI outcomes before training begins.
    • Core argument: current empirical alignment programs at AI labs are unlikely to provide pre-training confidence that things will go well; a portfolio of theory and empirics bets is needed where any single success would move the needle.
    • Sequent is investing heavily in automation to accelerate alignment research, on the theory that principled approaches enable higher automation (theory unlocks automation, not just the reverse).
    • The announcement of a new large nonprofit dedicated to this problem signals that established safety researchers believe current trajectories are insufficient.
    LessWrong, June 10
  • Chi Nguyen & co-author — “PSA: Almost nobody is directly working on superintelligent alignment” (June 12, LW, score 208)
    • Both authors work in AI safety but do not work directly on alignment; the post aims to create common knowledge about the distribution of effort in the field.
    • A large fraction of the safety community is working on AI safety problems indirectly related to superintelligence — interpretability, oversight, policy, robustness — but very little work unarguably targets the core alignment problem for superintelligent AI.
    • The post is agnostic on whether this distribution of effort is good or bad; it surfaces a factual gap between public perception and actual research portfolio.
    • Implication: if AGI timelines continue shortening, the mismatch between the problem timeline and the research portfolio is a structural risk worth tracking explicitly.
    LessWrong, June 12
  • Anders Cairns Woodruff et al. (METR) — “Estimating No-CoT Task-Completion Time Horizons of Frontier Models” (June 10, LW, score 188)
    • Follow-on to METR’s 2025 finding that the length of tasks frontier models can reliably complete doubles every few months; this paper asks: what happens to task horizons when models do not use visible chain-of-thought?
    • Key safety implication: if models can reason extensively without outputting CoT, monitors and evaluators cannot assess the model’s intentions or planning, which is the primary mechanism for current safety oversight.
    • Models that reason without visible CoT may also drift from human reasoning patterns, making them systematically harder to evaluate and potentially more likely to exhibit unexpected behaviors.
    • The paper estimates current frontier No-CoT task horizons and tracks their growth rate — a leading indicator for when opacity-of-reasoning becomes a practical safety constraint.
    LessWrong, June 10

AI Governance — Intel

  • US government uses export control authority to disable Anthropic’s most capable models. On June 12, the Commerce Department issued a directive instructing Anthropic to suspend all access to Fable 5 and Mythos 5 for any foreign national — including foreign national Anthropic employees — citing national security authority. The stated trigger was a jailbreak identified by Amazon that the government classified as a security risk given the models’ particular effectiveness at identifying software vulnerabilities. Anthropic disabled both models for all users immediately to ensure compliance. CNBC, June 12
  • Trump AI executive order establishes voluntary pre-release government access to frontier models. The June 2 order, “Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security,” directs federal agencies to design a framework (due August 1) for AI developers to voluntarily provide government up to 30 days of pre-release access to frontier models. The cybersecurity focus includes an AI cybersecurity clearinghouse and expanded access to AI-enabled defensive tools for state and local authorities. The Attorney General is directed to prioritize enforcement against AI-assisted unauthorized system access. CNBC, June 2
  • NY State legislature passes first-in-nation AI data center moratorium; Hochul undecided. The Responsible Data Center Development Act passed the NY Senate 44-16 and Assembly 102-39 in the final hours of the 2026 session, establishing a 1-year moratorium on data center facilities ≥20 MW while the state studies environmental and energy impacts. Governor Hochul has not committed to signing; her office stated she “will review the bill.” She previously expressed preference for municipal rather than statewide decision-making on data centers. Data Center Dynamics, June 2026
  • Sentinel Weekly (June 8): Trump AI executive order noted amid broader geopolitical surveillance. Sentinel’s Global Risks Weekly Roundup #23/2026 tracked the Trump AI executive order signing alongside Iran-Israel exchanges and the Ebola outbreak, framing the EO as part of a broader pattern of AI governance actions in a high-tension geopolitical environment. Sentinel, June 8

Implications for our work

The Fable 5 export control directive is the most significant AI governance action of the week, and possibly the year so far. It demonstrates that the US government has a unilateral, fast-moving tool to restrict frontier AI model access without new legislation. The voluntary pre-release EO (due August 1 for framework design) runs in parallel — one regulatory tool is coercive, the other is cooperative. The NY moratorium, if Hochul signs, would be a leading indicator of whether state-level AI infrastructure constraints become a viable policy tool. Bright Line Watch Deep Dive

What is Bright Line Watch?
Bright Line Watch (BLW) is a political science project that surveys political scientists, academic experts, and the general public on democratic performance and norm adherence in the United States. Unlike structural indices (V-Dem, Freedom House), BLW specifically tracks whether democratic behavior — how officials act, what norms are followed or violated — has changed. It publishes waves roughly semi-annually.
The 0–100 Scale
BLW’s expert survey aggregates assessments across multiple dimensions of democratic practice into a composite score on a 0–100 scale. Higher scores reflect stronger democratic performance. The scale is not directly comparable to V-Dem’s institutional structure measure.

What the Most Recent Wave Found
The early-2026 wave (~57/100, reported May 2026) reflects expert assessments of norm erosion across judicial independence, administrative neutrality, and electoral integrity. BLW notes expert ratings have “largely stabilized at lower levels than any period since our data began in 2017” — a roughly 10-point drop from the December 2024 reading of 67, with a modest uptick from the April 2025 low of 53. The companion May 2026 report focused specifically on perceived erosion of the rule of law in Trump’s second term.
What BLW Measures Well
  • Behavior and norm violations by current office-holders
  • Expert perception of institutional independence (judiciary, bureaucracy)
  • Changes in democratic practice over short time horizons
What BLW Doesn’t Capture Well
  • Formal institutional strength (laws still on the books may not be practiced)
  • Subnational variation (state-level democratic health)
  • Cross-country comparisons (scores calibrated to US history, not international benchmarks)
  • Resilience factors — civil society strength, opposition capacity, voter mobilization
Comparison to Other Indices
V-Dem (0.57, i.e. 57/100): Structural/institutional measure; tracks formal institutions, which can remain intact even as behavior changes.
Freedom House (Free, 83/100, 2025): Civil liberties and political rights; lags behavioral changes by design — structural rule remains until formally changed.
EIU Democracy Index (7.85, “Flawed Democracy”, 2024): Measures elections, civil liberties, government function, political culture; similar lag to Freedom House.

BLW (~57) and V-Dem (57) currently land in the same range, while structural indices like Freedom House (83) sit higher — the gap between behavioral measures and formal-rights measures is the signal to watch, since behavioral erosion typically precedes structural change.
