Democratic Resilience — Intel

  • Russia is targeting AI training data, not just social feeds. Olga Lautman documents a Russian influence operation (“Project 2026,” run by the sanctioned Social Design Agency) that builds Wikipedia clones, fake think tanks, and reference sites specifically to be scraped into AI training sets. The concrete proof point: a Ukrainian engineer found ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini all rendering occupied Crimea as “Russian” on generated maps, traced to a poisoned open-source dataset. This is a democracy-and-AI intersection worth tracking: the information-integrity threat now runs through model training, where it is harder to detect and correct than a social-media post. Olga Lautman, July 2
  • Press suppression is arriving through agency machinery, not headline censorship. Lautman’s framing: DOJ grand-jury subpoenas of NYT reporters, a joint Pentagon-DOJ leak task force, EEOC actions against the Times, and FCC pressure on broadcasters together form an architecture in which public delegitimization of journalists is the precondition that makes later agency actions politically survivable. The softening comes before the crackdown. Olga Lautman, July 14

Implications for our work

The SCOTUS term end split the difference for democratic resilience: two of the three rulings were structurally significant for presidential power (birthright loss for Trump, but a major win on firing independent-agency commissioners), while the mail-ballot ruling was a real and underappreciated win for ballot access heading into November. The more consequential near-term story is the DOJ posture toward election administration: the nationwide criminal-liability letters and the expansion of the Ohio probe to a national funder (America Votes) show the threat is to the funding-and-staffing infrastructure of voter-registration work, via subpoena and chilling effect rather than prosecution. The Fulton County subpoena being quashed shows the courts are still a live check. For field-building organizations with donor and volunteer records, this is the moment to review data-retention and legal-response protocols. The Russian AI-training-data operation is a new front worth a dedicated look, since it sits squarely at the democracy-and-AI intersection.

LessWrong & EA Forum — The “AI 2040: Plan A” Debate

  • Daniel Kokotajlo (AI Futures Project) — “AI 2040: Plan A” (July 9, LW)
    • The follow-up to AI 2027, framed as a recommendation rather than a prediction: “what we think should happen.”
    • Core claim: superintelligence is deliberately delayed to ~2040, versus ~2030 on the default trajectory, achieved by “decisive action on the part of the US and Chinese governments.”
    • Mechanism: a US-China deal to pause software progress at ~2030 (right before a software-driven intelligence explosion) while compute keeps scaling, with a “Consortium” reviewing which algorithms are too dangerous to implement.
    • Why it matters: the load-bearing variable is coordinated state action and international governance — directly the democracy-resilience-during-transition thesis.
    LessWrong, July 9
  • Richard Ngo — “Selective Optimism: a critique of AI 2040” (July 9, LW)
    • Argues Plan A’s 2040 handover to AIs is “extremely abrupt” — “there’s no way for most normal people to understand and consent to this process so quickly.”
    • A coordinated handover “seems to require that the process is being run by internationally-coordinated regulators, rather than letting the citizens of each country decide.”
    • Frames the core tension between the speed of the AI transition and democratic consent and legitimacy — the crux for this brief’s audience. (Ngo discloses he is a paid AIFP consultant.)
    LessWrong, July 9
  • Tom Davidson — “Plan A’s problem with dry tinder” (July 10, LW)
    • Quantified critique: pausing software while compute keeps scaling builds an overhang — roughly 100x compute by 2033 and another 100x by 2040, with each 10x of compute speeding an intelligence explosion ~5x.
    • So if the deal breaks in 2033 the intelligence explosion runs ~25x faster; in 2040, ~600x faster — compressing “a year” of takeoff into weeks or a single day.
    • Implication: a US-China pause could be destabilizing, because a single secret defector could gain a decisive strategic advantage within days, creating pre-emption incentives.
    LessWrong, July 10
  • djbinder — “The easiest pathway to control is through executive power” (July 10, LW)
    • Argues AI-takeover discussions overweight exotic mechanisms and underweight that power is already concentrated in the US President and Chinese General Secretary, who have the easiest means to use AI to entrench.
    • Mechanism: a rapid AI transition is an emergency, and emergencies default to the executive with wide discretion; courts defer on national security, cases are slow, and actions may be irreversible even if later ruled illegal.
    • Key point: much of what an executive would need to do “probably doesn’t require strongly illegal actions,” so subordinates wouldn’t refuse — the democracy-backsliding-meets-AI thesis stated from inside the safety community.
    LessWrong, July 10

Implications for our work

The Plan A debate is the most useful cluster this window because it makes the democracy-and-AI crux explicit and contested: the leading “good outcome” scenario depends on internationally-coordinated regulators running a fast handover, and its own critics (Ngo on democratic consent, Davidson on the compute overhang) identify exactly why that could be neither democratic nor stable. djbinder’s executive-power post is the sharpest statement of the concern that a rapid AI transition most naturally concentrates power in the executive. Meanwhile Sentinel’s China forecasts suggest the US-China coordination premise may be empirically shaky. For a democracy-field-building and AI-governance program, the actionable read is that governance-during-transition work should treat “who holds emergency executive authority” and “is coordination actually feasible” as first-order questions, not background assumptions.

Implications for our work

The Fable 5 episode is now a settled data point rather than an open crisis, and its lesson is the durable one: the executive branch has fast, unilateral tools over frontier AI access, bounded by politics rather than law. The most actionable near-term item is the GSA procurement comment window, where low participation means well-crafted comments can shape de facto federal AI standards — a high-leverage, low-cost intervention. On the field-building side, the Nardo (third-party model access) and Charbel-Raphaël (political-will bottleneck) posts point the same direction: outside-evaluator access and policymaker education are underfunded relative to their leverage, which is directly relevant to donor-strategy allocation. State-level action (NY) and international signaling (Xi’s UN-framework call) are both live channels worth tracking as the federal picture stays coercive-but-cooperative.

Bright Line Watch Deep Dive

What is Bright Line Watch?
Bright Line Watch (BLW) is a political science project that surveys political scientists, academic experts, and the general public on democratic performance and norm adherence in the United States. Unlike structural indices (V-Dem, Freedom House), BLW specifically tracks whether democratic behavior — how officials act, what norms are followed or violated — has changed. It publishes waves roughly semi-annually.
The 0–100 Scale
BLW’s expert survey aggregates assessments across multiple dimensions of democratic practice into a composite score on a 0–100 scale. Higher scores reflect stronger democratic performance. The scale is not directly comparable to V-Dem’s institutional structure measure.

What the Most Recent Wave Found
The early-2026 wave (~57/100, reported May 2026) reflects expert assessments of norm erosion across judicial independence, administrative neutrality, and electoral integrity. BLW notes expert ratings have “largely stabilized at lower levels than any period since our data began in 2017” — roughly a 10-point drop from the December 2024 reading of 67, with a modest uptick from the April 2025 low of 53. The companion May 2026 report (with UCLA Law’s Safeguarding Democracy Project) focused on perceived erosion of the rule of law in Trump’s second term.
What BLW Measures Well
  • Behavior and norm violations by current office-holders
  • Expert perception of institutional independence (judiciary, bureaucracy)
  • Changes in democratic practice over short time horizons
What BLW Doesn’t Capture Well
  • Formal institutional strength (laws still on the books may not be practiced)
  • Subnational variation (state-level democratic health)
  • Cross-country comparisons (scores calibrated to US history, not international benchmarks)
  • Resilience factors — civil society strength, opposition capacity, voter mobilization
Comparison to Other Indices
V-Dem (0.57, i.e. 57/100): Structural/institutional measure; tracks formal institutions, which can remain intact even as behavior changes.
Freedom House (Free, 83/100, 2025): Civil liberties and political rights; lags behavioral changes by design — structural rule remains until formally changed.
EIU Democracy Index (7.85, “Flawed Democracy”, 2024): Measures elections, civil liberties, government function, political culture; similar lag to Freedom House.

BLW (~57) and V-Dem (57) currently land in the same range, while structural indices like Freedom House (83) sit higher — the gap between behavioral measures and formal-rights measures is the signal to watch, since behavioral erosion typically precedes structural change.
